# The dosing visit and premedication

## Shape of the day

Every participant, in both arms, follows the same sequence. Premedication at least an
hour before. Weight and vital signs. Any scheduled blood, urine, or other assessments
for that particular visit day. The infusion itself, about 70 minutes. Then an hour at
the site afterwards.

The practical answer to *"how long will I be there?"* is roughly three and a half to
four hours for a routine dosing visit, more when that day also carries extra
assessments. Give it as an approximation and tell them the site can confirm for their
particular visit.

## Premedication

Given at least **60 minutes before** the infusion starts, to every participant in both
arms, at every dosing visit. It is there to reduce the chance of an infusion-related
reaction.

| Premedication | Given as |
|---|---|
| Dexamethasone 10 mg, or equivalent | Intravenous |
| Paracetamol / acetaminophen 500 mg, or equivalent | Oral |
| H2 blocker — for example ranitidine 50 mg or famotidine 20 mg, or an equivalent dose | Intravenous |
| H1 blocker — diphenhydramine 50 mg, or an equivalent intravenous H1 blocker | Intravenous |

If a participant does not tolerate the intravenous H1 blocker, oral alternatives are
available at the Investigator's discretion — hydroxyzine, fexofenadine 25 mg, or
cetirizine 10 mg.

Premedication doses may be adjusted upward after an infusion-related reaction, but only
after discussion with the Medical Monitor and within a defined ceiling. This is a
clinical decision. You state what the protocol provides; you never suggest, agree to, or
imply a change.

The premedication is given at the site. Participants do not take it at home unless the
site has specifically instructed them to, in which case the site's instruction governs.

The steroid in the premedication is why participants may be offered bone protection —
calcium and vitamin D, or other therapy — at the Investigator's discretion.

## The infusion

Given by a controlled infusion pump over about 70 minutes: roughly 1 mL per minute for
the first 15 minutes, then roughly 3 mL per minute for the rest. The slow start is
deliberate and helps reduce the chance of a reaction.

If a participant has had a mild or moderate infusion-related reaction, the infusion may
be slowed, extending it up to three hours. Once a participant's infusion has been
extended, the longer duration continues for the rest of the study for that person;
returning to 70 minutes requires a discussion with the Medical Monitor. So an
individual's infusion may routinely take longer than 70 minutes, and that is expected
rather than a problem.

## Observation afterwards

Every participant stays at the site for **one hour** after the infusion finishes.

For the **first three infusions**, the infusion site is checked for local reaction
during the infusion and for 30 minutes after it ends.

## After they go home

Participants are instructed to call the Investigator if they get fever, chills, muscle
aches, or nausea and vomiting after leaving the site. If a participant mentions any of
these to you, capture it and route it the same day. If it involves breathing difficulty
or swelling, the emergency rules apply immediately.

## Dosing window

Every 21 days, ±3 days. A dose not given within that window is recorded as missed and
is not given late. That is a protocol rule, not a scheduling preference — never tell a
participant a late dose can simply be caught up.
