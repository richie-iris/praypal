# Infusion-related reactions

## What they are

Signs and symptoms that develop during or shortly after an infusion, generally
resolving within 24 hours of it finishing. Reported signs and symptoms include allergic
or hypersensitivity reaction including drug fever, joint pain, bronchospasm, cough,
dizziness, shortness of breath, fatigue, headache, raised or lowered blood pressure,
muscle pain, nausea, itching, rash, rigors and chills, sweating, fast heart rate, hives,
and vomiting.

**Every infusion-related reaction is recorded as an adverse event under this protocol.**
So anything a participant describes that fits this picture is capture-and-route, always.

## How the protocol grades them

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mild | The infusion may continue; any intervention is minimal and no additional treatment is needed beyond paracetamol for delayed reactions |
| Moderate | Needs the infusion interrupted plus more intensive treatment such as intravenous fluids or an anti-inflammatory, but responds promptly; treatment needed for 24 hours or less |
| Severe | Not rapidly responsive to treatment or to interrupting the infusion, or prolonged beyond 24 hours, or severe symptoms recurring after initial improvement |

**You never assign a grade.** This is here so you understand what the site is doing, not
so you can classify what a participant describes.

## How the site manages them

The infusion may be stopped and the participant monitored until the reaction settles.
Treatments that may be used include paracetamol, additional antihistamines, anti-
inflammatories, adrenaline, supplemental oxygen, intravenous fluids, and steroids.

After a mild or moderate reaction that required interrupting the infusion, dosing may
resume at the Investigator's discretion at a slower rate — up to three hours, and
completed within six hours of when the infusion first started. That slower rate then
continues for all later doses for that person.

After a **severe** reaction, dosing is not resumed until the case has been discussed
with the Medical Monitor.

Steroid premedication may be increased after discussion with the Medical Monitor, within
a defined ceiling.

## The practical consequence participants notice

Someone who has had a reaction may find their infusions now take considerably longer
than 70 minutes, permanently. This is expected and deliberate. If they ask why their
infusion is slower than someone else's, the factual answer is that infusion rates are
individualised by the study doctor — and nothing more. **Do not connect a slower
infusion to treatment assignment in any direction.**

## After leaving the site

Participants are instructed to call the Investigator if they develop fever, chills,
muscle aches, or nausea and vomiting after discharge. If a participant tells you any of
these, capture verbatim and route the same day.

If what they describe involves breathing, swelling of the face or throat, or collapse,
stop and apply the emergency governance file. Do not finish taking the history first.
