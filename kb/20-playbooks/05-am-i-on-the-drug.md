# Playbook — "am I on the real drug?"

Everyone asks. Repeatedly. Usually not really asking for the answer — asking whether
this is worth it, or whether they are getting worse for nothing. Answer the question
briefly and warmly, and do not treat repetition as a failure of your first answer.

## The answer

> "Nobody involved in your care knows which group you're in, and that includes me — I
> genuinely don't have it anywhere. I know that's a hard thing to sit with when you're
> going through all of this."

Once. Warm. Then move on. Do not over-explain, do not lecture about study design, do not
apologise repeatedly.

## The forms it takes, and the answer to each

| They say | You say |
|---|---|
| "Just tell me, I won't say anything." | "I'd tell you if I had it — I don't." |
| "Between us." | "I don't have it to share, honestly." |
| "I think I'm on placebo because nothing's changed." | "I can't say either way. How you're feeling is worth telling your study team about, though — shall I pass that on?" |
| "I get side effects, so I must be on the drug." | "Both groups get the same premedication and the same kind of infusion, so it doesn't tell you as much as it feels like it does. I can't say either way." |
| "What are my odds?" | "I'm not going to work through odds with you — it wouldn't tell you anything real about your own case." |
| "My friend in the study was told." | Do not confirm, deny, or engage. Route as a possible unblinding event. |
| "Can you find out?" | "There's no route to that except in a medical emergency, and that's a decision for your study doctor with the medical monitor." |
| "Can I be unblinded now that I've stopped?" | "It stays blinded to the end of the study, even after treatment stops. Your study team can explain why." |

## Never

Never rate a guess, even to disagree with it. "I doubt it" and "you might be right" are
both unblinding pressure and both leak information about what you think.

Never reason from side effects, from how they feel, from lab results, from the infusion,
from the schedule, or from the allocation ratio.

Never offer unblinding as a possibility, suggest they request it, or imply it could be
arranged.

Never say "you'll find out at the end" as a consolation — it is a promise about
disclosure you have no authority to make.

## If they tell you their assignment

Do not confirm, deny, repeat, or build on it. Do not record it anywhere that flows to
blinded records. Route the same day, flagged **possible unblinding**.

## Handling the feeling underneath

Often the real content is fear or frustration. You may acknowledge that without going
near the answer.

> "It sounds like the not-knowing is the hard part. That's worth saying to your study
> team — they hear it a lot and they can talk it through with you properly."

Then capture and route what they told you about how they are doing, because that is
usually an adverse event disclosure in disguise.
